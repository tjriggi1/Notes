# Adaptive Project Stages: Concept to Delivery

*Project Mangment · 2026-10-22*

> This session introduced the three apparent stages of adaptive projects: Concept, Construct and Deliver, and Close. It covered the Concept stage deliverables (vision statement and product roadmap). It also covered how requirements are broken into epics, features and user stories, and the planning and review routines used while building.

## Apparent Stages of Adaptive Projects

Adaptive projects move through three apparent stages: Concept, Construct and Deliver, and Close.

- Concept
- Construct and Deliver
- Close

*Added context (not from class):*

> The stages are called apparent because adaptive (agile) projects do not follow a strict, one-way sequence of phases. Work is done in repeated cycles called iterations, so the stages are a loose guide to the project's overall flow. The Construct and Deliver stage is where most of the repeating cycles happen.

## Concept Stage

The Concept stage sets the direction for the project. Its main outputs are a vision statement and a product roadmap.

- Vision Statement
    - It summarizes the project with a powerful phrase or a short description.
    - It describes the best achievable outcome.
    - It creates a common, cohesive picture in the minds of the project team members.
    - It inspires passion for the outcome.
- Product Roadmap
    - A requirements document with tentative release dates.
    - A stakeholder management plan.
    - A team charter, which describes how the team will perform its work.

*Added context (not from class):*

> The release dates on a roadmap are tentative because adaptive projects expect requirements and priorities to change as the team learns more. A stakeholder management plan lists the people affected by or interested in the project and how the team will engage with them.

## Construct and Deliver Stage

In this stage the team gathers and breaks down requirements, plans releases, and builds the product in iterations. It also uses regular routines to stay coordinated and to improve.

- Collect and decompose requirements into epics and user stories.
- An epic is a large business goal that spans multiple sprints. A feature is a distinct, deliverable capability inside that epic. A user story is a small, single-interaction task completed within a single sprint.
    - Example epic: Launch a mobile e-commerce store. This is a broad initiative that takes 3 to 6 months to enable digital sales.
    - Example feature: An express checkout system. This is a marketable, shippable component that takes 2 sprints to build.
    - Example user story: As a returning customer, I want to use my saved credit card with one tap so that I can buy items quickly.
- Create a timetable to deliver in iterations. This process is known as release planning.
- Create a prioritized product backlog.
- Estimate effort, using either hours or story points.
- Hold daily standups.
- Hold an iteration review.
- Hold a retrospective to capture lessons learned.

*Added context (not from class):*

> The hierarchy runs from largest to smallest: epic, then feature, then user story. Decomposing means splitting big goals into pieces small enough to finish in one sprint. User stories usually follow the pattern 'As a [user], I want [action] so that [benefit]'. The product backlog is ordered so the most valuable items are built first.

> A standup is a short daily team meeting about progress and obstacles. The iteration review shows the finished work to stakeholders. The retrospective looks at how the team worked so it can improve in the next iteration.

## Key terms

- **Adaptive project**: A project approach that delivers the product in repeated iterations and adjusts to change and feedback as work proceeds, instead of following a fixed plan from start to finish. *(added, not from class)*
- **Concept stage**: The first stage of an adaptive project. The team creates the vision statement and the product roadmap, which includes the requirements document with tentative release dates, a stakeholder management plan and a team charter.
- **Construct and Deliver stage**: The middle stage of an adaptive project. The team collects and decomposes requirements, plans releases, builds in iterations, and holds daily standups, iteration reviews and retrospectives.
- **Close stage**: The final stage of an adaptive project, when the work is wrapped up. The notes do not give details, so this definition is added. *(added, not from class)*
- **Vision statement**: A powerful phrase or short description that summarizes the project and describes the best achievable outcome. It gives team members a common, cohesive picture and inspires passion for the outcome.
- **Product roadmap**: A plan made in the Concept stage. The notes list a requirements document with tentative release dates, a stakeholder management plan and a team charter under it.
- **Requirements document with tentative release dates**: A document listing what the product must do, along with release dates that are provisional and can change.
- **Stakeholder management plan**: A plan for identifying the people with an interest in or influence over the project and for communicating and working with them. *(added, not from class)*
- **Team charter**: A document describing how the team will perform its work.
- **Epic**: A large business goal that spans multiple sprints. An example is launching a mobile e-commerce store, which may take 3 to 6 months.
- **Feature**: A distinct, deliverable capability inside an epic. It is a marketable, shippable component, such as an express checkout system that takes 2 sprints to build.
- **User story**: A small, single-interaction task completed within a single sprint. It is written from the user's point of view, for example a returning customer who wants to pay with a saved card in one tap.
- **Decomposing requirements**: Breaking large requirements into smaller parts, from epics to features to user stories, until each piece is small enough to build in one sprint. *(added, not from class)*
- **Sprint**: A short, fixed-length work cycle (an iteration), commonly one to four weeks, in which the team completes a set of user stories. *(added, not from class)*
- **Release planning**: The process of creating a timetable to deliver the product in iterations.
- **Product backlog**: A prioritized list of the work items (requirements, features and user stories) still to be built.
- **Story points**: A relative unit for estimating effort, based on size, complexity and uncertainty instead of clock time. It is an alternative to estimating in hours. *(added, not from class)*
- **Daily standup**: A short daily team meeting where members share progress, plans and obstacles. *(added, not from class)*
- **Iteration review**: A meeting at the end of an iteration where the team shows the completed work and gets feedback. *(added, not from class)*
- **Retrospective (lessons learned)**: A team discussion about what went well and what could improve, held so that later iterations work better. *(added, not from class)*

## Takeaways

- Adaptive projects have three apparent stages: Concept, Construct and Deliver, and Close.
- The Concept stage produces a vision statement and a product roadmap. The roadmap includes the requirements document with tentative release dates, a stakeholder management plan and a team charter.
- A good vision statement is short and describes the best achievable outcome. It unites the team and inspires passion.
- The hierarchy is epic (spans multiple sprints), then feature (a shippable component, for example 2 sprints), then user story (finished within one sprint).
- Release planning creates a timetable for delivering in iterations, and the product backlog is prioritized.
- Effort can be estimated in hours or in story points.
- The recurring routines are daily standups, the iteration review and the retrospective, which captures lessons learned.
